King + 2 Pawns vs King + Pawn

The King + 2 Pawns vs King + Pawn endgame is another endgame where one side is up a pawn, but the result will depend on king position. Very often, this endgame will end up in a King + Pawn vs King endgame as discussed in the previous section.

With an extra pawn for both sides, there are more possibilities and methods to outplay the opponent. For the side with the pawn advantage, they will wish to trade a pair of pawns and enter a winning endgame with one pawn. In certain positions, the extra pawn may also be used for waiting moves, helping to win the opponent's pawn for an easy win with two extra pawns. For the defending side, the goal is simply to prevent these ideas, and try to trade the pawns in such a way to reach a drawn endgame down a pawn.

With this being said, let us explore this endgame by considering the following position:

Figure 1.0: White to Move

Considering this position, it is clear how the additional pawns provide White with a much larger advantage than if they only had one pawn. With the pawn on g6 pinning down Black's pawn and king, White can simply march their king forward and trade the pawns when they wish to obtain a winning endgame.

To do this, White should first get their pieces to the most optimal positions before trading pawns, so a good idea here would be to improve the king by playing 1.Ke6. Since Black cannot really move their pieces, they will just shuffle, and a potential line would be 1...Kf8 2.Kd7 Kg8 3.Ke7 Kh8, reaching the following position:

Figure 1.1: Position after 3...Kh8

Note that White cannot further improve the position of their king because 4.Kf7?? would result in stalemate. Now that the king is in the best position possible, we can start pushing our pawn to break through. Thus, we should play 4.f5!, which will be responded to with Kg8, Black's only move. Now, White has some options:

Figure 1.2: Position after 4...Kg8

White now has to be careful, because 5.f6?? will draw by stalemate after 5...gxf6 6.Kxf6 Kf8, reaching a theoretical drawn endgame discussed in the previous chapter. Thus, White has to make a waiting move to force the king away, with 5.Ke8!! Kh8 6.f6 gxf6 7.Kf7 leading to a winning position for White, with promotion coming in the next two moves.

From the previous example, it can be seen how the extra pawn is a lot stronger for White, and this endgame is thus winning most of the time. To see this in another example, consider the following position:

Figure 2.0: White to Move

Notice how in this position, Black's pawn and king are in a more active position, but the White's b-pawn is already ahead of Black's pawn. Thus, White's goal in this position will be to try and outmaneuver Black's king and capture the a-pawn.

To go for Black's pawn, White must approach it with the king, so 1.Kc4 is a very natural move. Black must also go to try and defend it, so 1...Kc7 is a normal reply as well, leading to this position:

Figure 2.1: Position after 1...Kc7

White has two main options in this position:

  • 2.Kc5 actually does manage to win in this position as White's pawn promotes faster after 2...Kb7 (3.b6?? Ka6 4.Kc6 is stalemate!) 3.Kd6 Kb6 4.Kd7! Kc5 5.Kc7 Kb4 6.Kb6 Kxa4 7.Ka6 Kb4 8.b6 a4 9.b7 a3 10.a8=Q+ results in an easily winning endgame up a queen for White
  • 2.Kd5 is the strategical approach to win the a-pawn, because after 2...Kb6 (2...Kb7 3.Kc5 transposes) 3.Kd6 Kb7 4.Kc5, leading to the following position:

Figure 2.2: Position after 4.Kc5

Black has two options here, but is losing in both of them no matter what:

  • 4...Kc7 loses after 5.b6+ Kb7 6.Kb5, with White simply taking the pawn on a5 on the next move
  • 4...Ka7 is the slightly trickier approach, but it still wins after 5.Kc6! (5.b6+? Ka6 6.Kc6 is stalemate) Kb8 6.Kb6, winning the a5 pawn on the next move

Thus, White has won easily in the past two examples, and it can be seen how it is much easier to win with an extra pawn on the board as discussed in this section.
